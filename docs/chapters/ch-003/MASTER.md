## 02 · English Connection

English vowel spelling is often unpredictable. German has useful regularities, but spelling alone is not a flawless sound guide. German long vowels are normally steady: avoid sliding them into English-style diphthongs.

| German | English | Sound connection |
|---|---|---|
| Name | name | Long German a: steady vowel |
| Mann | man | Short a, often distinct from long a |
| See | lake | Long e represented by ee |
| Boot | boat | Long o is steady, not English oh plus glide |
| Haus | house | au resembles the vowel movement in English now, accent dependent |

## 04 · Short & Long Vowels

Vowel length can change meaning. Listen to the contrast: Stadt /ʃtat/ (city) vs Staat /ʃtaːt/ (state). German vowels can be short or long; length often comes with a change in vowel quality, not just duration.

| Short | Long | What to notice |
|---|---|---|
| Stadt — city | Staat — state | /a/ versus /aː/ |
| offen — open | Ofen — oven | /ɔ/ versus /oː/ |
| bitten — to request | bieten — to offer | /ɪ/ versus /iː/ |

## 05 · Sound-Spelling Patterns

Helpful clues, not universal rules: doubled consonants often follow a short vowel (Bett, Mutter); ie commonly signals long /iː/ (Liebe); doubled vowels often signal length (See, Boot); h can mark a long vowel (Zahl). Always check the word and exceptions.

| Pattern | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| tt after vowel | Bett | bed |
| ie | Liebe | love |
| ee | See | lake |
| oo | Boot | boat |
| vowel + h | Zahl | number |

## 08 · Umlauts Ä Ö Ü

The umlauts ä, ö and ü represent vowel qualities, not decorative marks. The mouth can help: for an ö sound, form rounded lips while making a front-vowel sound; for ü, round the lips while keeping the tongue forward as for ee.

| Word | Meaning | Listening target |
|---|---|---|
| schön | beautiful / nice | /ʃøːn/ · long ö |
| fünf | five | /fʏnf/ · short ü |
| Tür | door | /tyːɐ̯/ · long ü |
| Mädchen | girl | /ˈmɛːtçən/ · long ä |
| Äpfel | apples | /ˈɛpfəl/ · short ä |

## 09 · English vs German

Compare English and German: English speakers may say German ü like English oo, turn a long German e into the two-part vowel in English day, or turn German o into English oh. These substitutes hide German sound contrasts.

| German | Common English-speaker trap | Better listening goal |
|---|---|---|
| See | say with glide | steady /eː/ |
| gut | English good | long /uː/ |
| schön | English shone | rounded front /øː/ |
| Tür | English tour | rounded front /yː/ |

## 10 · Common Mistake Alert

Common mistakes: (1) making long vowels glide like English day; (2) saying ü as English oo; (3) guessing stress from English; (4) reading ie as two separate sounds; (5) treating ä, ö and ü as interchangeable. Listen, compare, retry.

Accuracy beats speed. For unfamiliar vowels, start with a short word, hear it multiple times and repeat with controlled mouth shape.

## 11 · Memory Trick

Word stress: in many everyday German words, the first syllable is stressed, but this is not universal. Notice NA-me /ˈnaːmə/, MUT-ter /ˈmʊtɐ/, LIE-be /ˈliːbə/; in be-KOM-men /bəˈkɔmən/ the second syllable is stressed. Capital letters in the cues below show stress, not German spelling.

Memory trick: IE says /iː/ as in Liebe; EI says /aɪ̯/ as in mein. AU says /aʊ̯/ as in Haus; EU and ÄU commonly say /ɔʏ̯/ as in neu and Häuser.

| Spelling / stress | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ie | Liebe | love |
| ei | mein | my |
| au | Haus | house |
| eu | neu | new |
| äu | Häuser | houses |
| first-syllable stress | Mutter | mother |
| second-syllable stress | bekommen | to receive |
